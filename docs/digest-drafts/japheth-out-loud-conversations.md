Pent-up emotions need an outlet.

Some things weigh too much to keep inside, so Listening Ears set them free under one theme: “Out Loud, conversations we don’t usually have.” Behind every smile is a story that deserves to be heard. The event was held on Thursday 26 November 2026 at the Nursing Smart Class, where all AUO students were invited to say the things they usually keep to themselves. Outside, a poster stood by the door, and beside it a board was already crowded with sticky notes, each one a line someone had left behind before walking in. It did not start with a lecture. It started with the room.

The room comes together

Diamond Osinmade’s first move was to take three rows of people and fold them into one. It was deliberate, and it deserves more credit than it gets: she unified the room before the hard questions began. Subconsciously, it puts everybody in the same headspace for what came next, so that by the time the speakers got to the hard questions, people were already listening.

Pressure that does not look like the dictionary

Academic pressure was the next thread, and the room was not using the dictionary meaning. It was the personal one: what it feels like to carry the work, the grades, and the future around with you.

Some students said they felt almost none. One said the family trusts him to do what is best for himself, so the pressure does not come from home. Others felt a small push to perform, the same way Olaleye Precious does. One speaker talked about measuring herself against roommates and feeling behind, even while doing the work. The distinction that came up again and again was between self-imposed pressure and external pressure. And a third thread: thinking too far ahead. When you are always planning for the future, the present starts to feel like a test you are already failing.

Separating expectations from self-worth

Still with the first speaker, the room was asked to sort their expectations into two piles: the ones they put on themselves and the ones that come from outside. Most people found the first pile was bigger.

Then came the question everyone had an opinion on: money or impact? Some picked money and called it the holy grail. Some picked impact and said it was the priority. Most settled somewhere in the middle. Someone mentioned knowledge. The room pushed back: knowledge towards what? Impact or towards money? He chose money. Another student said the word that mattered to her was relevance. She talked about planning toward a goal, then taking small, actionable steps toward it.

She left the room with one question: are you overwhelmed by the tasks you have to do, or by the person you have to become?

You don’t have to carry it alone

The second speaker was Olaleye Precious. His line was simple and direct: you don’t have to carry it alone. He pointed out that you have to notice you are carrying something before you can ask for help.

He talked about the burdens students were carrying, and about how people cope. Some run early in the morning. Some listen to the same music for hours. He asked the room to take a breath and name those burdens, not as anything special, but as students, as humans, from different walks of life and different departments.

He also made a point that is easy to miss: distress is not always visible. It can be quiet, even hidden from the people closest to us. Triggers often come from disappointment with academic expectations. Physical problems are easier to see. Mental problems need someone to talk to, and someone who will listen. “We cannot afford to be selfish and close ourselves off to the people around us so they can talk to us,” he said, and the same goes the other way.

Having someone to trust, he argued, is far better than the easy alternative: drugs and other vices that help people cope for an hour and never offer a lasting answer.

He left the room with a challenge: if you want to be a good listener, stop asking “how are you doing?” and start asking “how are you doing, really?”

Self-esteem and how we see ourselves

Once both talks were done, the room split into three groups. Students picked a number, 1, 2 or 3, and moved in intervals: ones to the first group, twos to the second, threes to the third. I was in the third group. In those groups, the room talked about self-perception: the highs, the lows, and the times we did not like ourselves. Students shared some hard moments. One said they always competed with themselves and felt like they were failing, because of a picture of success they had set for themselves. Another said she felt that her good was never good enough. Another spoke about struggling to make friends in the early school years, feeling secluded, and then slowly learning to be more social. One student spoke about feeling out of step with what people expected of him, even back in school. [CONFIRM: wording with the speaker before publish]

Two things came up when the groups came back together. The first was that talking to people is one of the most practical ways to fight low self-esteem. The second was about separating what is internal from what is not. Habits and thought patterns can be worked on. Physical features cannot always be changed. The room came back to self-love and confidence as the answer to the second kind.

Separating expectations from self-worth, which the first speaker had already opened, came back here. The groups were sorting the same split: what we put on ourselves, and what we take in from outside. The exercise itself is above. This was the room returning to it.

Who was in the room

The event was hosted by Igili Bernice and held in collaboration with Passizy, which sponsored the event alongside the organizers. The theme came with a promise: a safe space where no voice is overlooked and no feeling is dismissed, which the organizers framed through Listening Ears. A Passizy representative was then introduced on stage to present the product: timetables, course notes, and flashcards in one place, so students can plan their study week without juggling apps and chats.

The speakers were Olaleye Precious, also President AUSA 2026-2027 session, and Diamond Osinmade. Other AUSA executives were present, including the PRO, Tobiloba Jimba.

Why this matters

Campus talk usually runs on the surface: the fees, the timetable, the lecturers. Out Loud went under that. It is worth saying out loud that the pressure many students feel is real, that it is often self-made, and that carrying it alone is optional.

If the afternoon stays with you, two doors are already on campus. Passizy, a platform for timetables, course notes, and study plans, was part of the call to action. The school also has a counseling unit.
